The best way to prevent AdBlue problems is unglamorous: buy AdBlue made to ISO 22241 from a supplier you trust, keep it clean, don't run the tank down to the last warning, and act on the first fault message rather than the countdown. Add a simple refill and warning record for each vehicle, and a fault can end as a planned repair rather than as a van that won't restart on a customer's drive.

None of this needs special products. Looking after an AdBlue system is about not giving it the things that harm it: wrong or dirty fluid, a dry tank, cold short runs and ignored warnings.

> The easiest repair to plan is the one the first warning gave you time to book.

## How to prevent AdBlue problems: start with the right fluid

The standard matters more than the brand. What counts is that the fluid meets ISO 22241-1, the quality standard for AUS 32, the generic name for AdBlue: a set mix of urea in high-purity water. Many handbooks ask for the standard rather than a brand. Mazda, for example, accepts its own product or any AdBlue conforming to ISO 22241-1.

AdBlue is a VDA trademark, and licensed makers must meet ISO 22241. But the VDA warns that some fluid is sold under the name without a licence. No label tells you how it has been stored since. Buy from suppliers you trust, and avoid anything decanted into unmarked containers.

## Storage and handling in vans and depots

- **Temperature and shelf life.** Yara recommends storing it between 0 and 30°C, out of direct sunlight, and says sealed AdBlue keeps for up to a year that way. BASF's technical leaflet gives 18 months from production at product temperatures between minus 11 and 25°C. The ranges differ, but the rule is the same: urea slowly breaks down into ammonia, faster as it warms, so the cooler the stock, the longer it lasts. Cold doesn't spoil it. It freezes at about minus 11°C and is fine once thawed, as our post on [AdBlue in winter](/blog/adblue-in-winter-freezing-and-heater-faults) explains.
- **Sealed and clean.** Keep containers closed when not in use, and the storage area clean and free of dust. Anything that has held fuel or oil stays away from AdBlue stock: Yara says even very small traces of fuel, oil or lubricant can damage the SCR system.
- **Bulk tanks and fittings.** BASF lists non-alloyed steel, zinc-coated (galvanised) steel, copper and copper alloys as unsuitable for AdBlue. It names austenitic stainless steels (the chromium-nickel and chromium-nickel-molybdenum grades) as suitable, and polymers such as polyethylene and polypropylene up to 60°C, ideally with the supplier's written resistance data. Brass is a copper alloy, so avoid brass fittings. ISO 22241-3 covers handling, transport and storage.
- **Rotate stock.** Use the oldest first and opened containers before sealed ones, and check the date on the pack, especially stock that has spent a summer in a van.
- **Keep the filler clean.** Wipe round the cap before opening and refit it straight away, so road dirt doesn't fall into the tank.

## Refill habits

- **Don't run it to the last warning.** Cars and lighter vans have to warn well before the tank is empty, and the warning escalates as it gets closer, so there is plenty of notice. Top up at the first message, not when a countdown starts.
- **Use the method your handbook allows.** Dedicated packs, or a pump nozzle that fits your vehicle's filler, never a shared funnel. Some handbooks ask for an anti-spill bottle rather than a forecourt dispenser: Mazda's handbook warns that a dispenser can cause leakage or spray.
- **Decide who refills.** Drivers at the pump, the depot from bulk, or the dealer at service: pick one and write it down, so nobody assumes someone else topped up.

## Duty cycle: multi-drop, short runs and idling

Urea only turns into ammonia properly in a hot exhaust. The ICCT says it needs at least 180°C and notes that light-duty exhausts in urban driving are usually below 200°C. Multi-drop work, short hops between jobs and long idling can keep the exhaust near or below that line, so deposits can form faster than heat clears them. Our post on [AdBlue crystallisation and dosing faults](/blog/adblue-crystallisation-and-dosing-faults) explains the mechanism.

Where the work allows, a regular longer run at steady speed lets the exhaust get properly hot. If a van's work is all short hops, say so when you report a fault, because it can change what gets checked first.

## Act on the first warning

An AdBlue fault warning is a deadline. Under the Euro 6 type-approval rules for cars and lighter vans, a quality or dosing fault that isn't put right within 50 km (about 30 miles) of the warning brings restrictions, which on many vehicles means a countdown to no restart. Heavier vans and trucks approved under the heavy-duty Euro VI rules have their own warning and inducement stages. Our guide to the [AdBlue countdown and no-start](/adblue-countdown-no-start) explains what happens if it is left.

- Don't clear codes before diagnosis. Clearing wipes the freeze-frame data that shows when and how the fault set, and the warning returns if the fault is still there.
- If several vehicles show quality or efficiency faults around the same time, check the shared AdBlue supply first.

## Software updates and manufacturer bulletins

Not every SCR fault is a failed part. Manufacturers issue software updates and technical bulletins for SCR problems on particular models, and our guide to [fault code P20EE](/adblue-fault-codes/p20ee) lists US bulletins, some where the fix was a reprogram rather than a part. At each service, ask whether any updates or campaigns are outstanding for that VIN.

## Records and a driver briefing for every vehicle

### Keep records

Diagnosis is quicker when there is history to go on. For each vehicle, keep:

- **A refill log:** date, mileage, litres and the source of the AdBlue. Our post on [how much AdBlue a vehicle should use](/blog/how-much-adblue-should-a-vehicle-use) shows how to turn it into each vehicle's normal, so a change stands out.
- **A warning history:** date, a photo of each dash message, any codes, the outside temperature and what was done.
- **Repair and software history:** parts fitted, updates applied and who did the work.

The vehicle keeps a record too. On cars and lighter vans, the type-approval rules require the reason for any inducement, such as a restart countdown, to be stored in a non-erasable form. Your notes add what the vehicle can't know: where the fluid came from and what the van was doing.

### Driver briefing points

- Only AdBlue goes in the AdBlue tank: no water, no additives, nothing to get home on.
- Use sealed packs, or the bottle or nozzle your handbook allows, never a container or funnel that has held anything else.
- Check the cap before you pour.
- Wear gloves: Yara recommends them to prevent irritation to sensitive skin.
- Top up at the first refill message.
- Photograph any AdBlue or emissions message and report it the same day.
- Don't drive on through a countdown without telling the office how many miles are left.
- If the wrong fluid goes in, either way round, don't start the engine. Call the office first: our post covers [what to do about diesel in the AdBlue tank](/blog/diesel-in-the-adblue-tank).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best AdBlue to use?

Any AdBlue that meets ISO 22241, from a supplier you trust. The standard and how the fluid has been stored matter more than the brand, but check your handbook for anything more your manufacturer asks for.

### How long can you store AdBlue?

Yara says up to a year if it is kept sealed between 0 and 30°C and out of direct sunlight, and BASF's technical leaflet gives 18 months from production at up to 25°C. Heat shortens it, so don't keep stock on a van floor through the summer. Those figures are for sealed stock, so reseal an opened container straight away and use it first.

### Can we fill vans from a bulk AdBlue tank at the depot?

Yes, as long as the tank and fittings are made of suitable materials, the tank stays sealed and clean, and the stock turns over. Ask the supplier to confirm the materials, and have the bulk supply tested if several vans develop quality faults.

### Should I use an additive to stop AdBlue crystals?

No. Handbooks say to put nothing but AdBlue in the tank: Mazda's, for example, says not to add any fluid other than AdBlue or dilute it with water. Crystals usually point to a leak, cold short runs or a dosing fault, so find the cause rather than treating the fluid.

## The takeaway

Prevention is mostly habit: the right fluid stored properly, refills at the first message, warnings reported the same day and a record for every vehicle. Do that, and when a fault does appear it is caught early and diagnosed quickly.
