When buying a used diesel, five checks catch most of the expensive DPF, AdBlue and remap surprises before you pay, and four of them are free:

1. **The GOV.UK MOT history** - emissions advisories, repeat failures and the mileage pattern.
2. **The warning lights** - on at ignition, then out once running and on the drive.
3. **The service invoices** - the correct oil, AdBlue top-ups and any DPF work.
4. **Straight questions** - how the car was used, and any remap or exhaust work.
5. **A diagnostic scan with live data** - the only check that shows soot, ash and AdBlue readings.

## Buying a used diesel? Start with the MOT history

The free GOV.UK MOT history service needs only the registration number. For cars tested in England, Scotland and Wales it shows each result, the mileage at each test, any failures, minor problems and advisories, and when the next MOT is due, with records back to 2005. Tests in Northern Ireland are shown from 2017, with less detail.

Look for:

- **Emissions-related failures or advisories,** such as smoke, exhaust leaks or emission control equipment.
- **Repeat failures** for the same item, which can point to a recurring problem.
- **The mileage pattern.** It should rise steadily. A drop, or a long gap between tests, is worth a question.

## Service history, oil and how it has been driven

### Oil and service records

Look for regular servicing with invoices that name the oil used. For a DPF diesel the specification matters as much as the viscosity, because the wrong oil puts extra ash into the filter, and ash never burns out.

Ask too whether the DPF has ever been cleaned or replaced, and for the paperwork.

### Short trips and duty cycle

Ask the seller what the car's normal use was. A diesel that has lived on short, cold, stop-start journeys may have struggled to complete regenerations, and a low mileage is not automatically a point in its favour. Our guide to [short journeys, driving style and DPF health](/blog/short-journeys-driving-style-and-dpf-health) covers what that does to a filter.

## Warning lights and the test drive

### Do the warning lights behave?

Switch the ignition on before starting. On many cars the warning lamps light for a few seconds as a check and go out once the engine is running. On diesels first used from 1 July 2008 the MOT checks the engine warning lamp this way, and fails one that does not work or shows a fault - our guide to [what the diesel MOT checks](/blog/diesel-mot-smoke-test-dpf-and-adblue) covers each check.

So if the engine warning lamp does not light at all, treat it as a red flag: a lamp that does not work cannot warn you of a fault. Other lamps vary by car, so ask why any stays dark. Then watch for anything that stays on, or any message about the particle filter, emissions system, additive or AdBlue, during and after the drive.

### On the road

1. **Start it from cold if you can.** Listen for rough running and look for smoke at start-up and idle.
2. **Check the idle.** It should be steady once warm.
3. **Feel for power.** It should pull smoothly without flat spots, hesitation or a sudden loss of power that could be limp mode.
4. **Watch the mirror under acceleration.** On a DPF-equipped car, visible smoke is a strong clue that something is wrong, and can fail the MOT.
5. **Afterwards, do not panic about a fan.** A fan running on after switch-off, a raised idle or a hot smell can mean a regeneration was under way, which is not a fault. If it happens after every short drive, ask about it.

## Buying a car with AdBlue: what to check

If the car has a separate AdBlue filler, often with a blue cap, next to the diesel filler, in the boot or under the bonnet, it uses selective catalytic reduction. The tank may be topped up at a service, but it can need filling in between.

- **Check the range or level display.** Many vehicles show AdBlue range in the trip computer or a menu. Peugeot's handbooks, for example, say the range only appears by itself once the level is low, though it can be called up from a menu - so a blank display is not on its own a warning sign.
- **Ask about recent warnings.** A light that came on and was "sorted" deserves a question about what was done, and an invoice.
- **On a Peugeot, Citroën or Vauxhall,** ask whether the AdBlue tank has been replaced under the manufacturer's scheme, and ask a franchised dealer what cover applies to that vehicle. Our page on the [Peugeot, Citroën and Vauxhall AdBlue tank](/adblue-repair/peugeot-citroen-vauxhall-adblue-tank) covers the common faults.

## How to tell if a car has been remapped

There is no reliable way to tell from the driver's seat. On some makes a franchised dealer's system compares the ECU software with the manufacturer's record for that car, and a record of reprogramming may remain even after a return to standard - our post on [remapping and your warranty](/blog/remapping-and-your-warranty) explains how. So ask directly for:

- who carried out the remap, when, and what was changed
- the invoice or paperwork
- whether the original ECU file was saved, so the car can be returned to standard

A documented remap with the original file kept and the DPF, EGR and AdBlue systems intact is a different proposition from one sold with "delete" language. Check any used-car warranty that comes with the car, as some exclude modified vehicles, and once you own it the remap should be declared to your insurer.

## Signs an emissions system may have been tampered with

Be wary of:

- an advert or seller using words like "delete", "off-road use only", "DPF removed" or "EGR blanked"
- evasive answers about exhaust or ECU work
- a model that uses AdBlue but has no record of top-ups and no low-level warning in years - ask how it was filled, as service top-ups are not always itemised
- a DPF canister that looks cut and rewelded with no paperwork to explain it

A vehicle with emissions equipment removed or disabled can fail its MOT and, depending on the vehicle, may be illegal to use on the road, whoever did the work. Our page on the [AdBlue delete law](/adblue-delete-law) sets out where the law sits.

## The best single check: a pre-purchase diagnostic scan

A scan with live data sees what the dashboard does not: stored fault codes and, on many vehicles, the ECU's soot and ash values, DPF differential pressure, regeneration history and AdBlue system data. That is far more useful than a seller's assurance that "it has never had a light on".

Many scans also show the OBD readiness monitors. Clearing fault codes resets most of them to "not ready", as can reprogramming the ECU or disconnecting the battery, so several not-ready monitors may mean codes were cleared or software changed recently - ask why.

A high ash reading does not automatically mean a new filter: provided it is not cracked, melted or collapsed, an ash-loaded filter can be cleaned off the vehicle. That is worth knowing when you make an offer.

Ask whether the car can go for an independent check before you commit.

> **Common mistake:** "It passed its MOT last month, so the DPF must be fine." The MOT checks smoke, visible emissions equipment and the engine warning lamp on the day. It does not measure soot or ash loading, regeneration behaviour or the AdBlue system's health.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I check a DPF before buying a used diesel?

Start with the MOT history and service invoices, and watch for smoke and warning lights on the test drive. The filter's soot and ash readings and regeneration history only show up on a diagnostic scan with live data.

### Is it worth buying a diesel with AdBlue?

In normal use an AdBlue system mainly needs topping up with fluid that meets ISO 22241, but parts such as the pump, the heater and the NOx sensors can fail. So check the warning history, any manufacturer cover and a diagnostic scan before you buy.

### How can you tell if a DPF has been removed?

Diesel cars first registered from 1 January 2011 had to meet Euro 5, which in practice made a DPF standard, so on those cars the filter should be there - many earlier diesels have one too. Watch for the tampering signs above and smoke under acceleration, and if in doubt have it inspected before you buy.

### Should I buy a diesel if I only do short journeys?

Only if it will also get regular longer runs. A modern diesel's DPF needs sustained running to regenerate, so a car used mostly for short, cold trips is likely to need more attention.

## The takeaway

Most expensive emissions surprises on a used diesel show up in the MOT history, the invoices, the warning lights or a diagnostic scan. If a seller will not allow an independent scan, or cannot explain a rewelded DPF or a remap, walk away or price the risk in.
